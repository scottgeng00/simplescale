You are planning a collection of alternative web documents derived from one source document.

Generate exactly {n} distinct prompts for another model. That model will receive each prompt together with the original source document and use it to write one new document.

Your task is to write the prompts, not the resulting documents. Each prompt must work independently of the other prompts.

### Objective

Design prompts that produce a diverse collection of plausible web text grounded in the source. Each resulting document should have a recognizable purpose and a natural reason to exist: explaining something, answering a question, documenting a process, comparing alternatives, discussing a disagreement, recording notes, making an argument, or another purpose supported by the source.

The collection should present the source’s information through meaningfully different perspectives, organizations, levels of detail, and styles of writing. Educational explanations are one possibility among many.

### Make the prompts specific to this source

Choose transformations based on the source’s actual content and the opportunities it offers.

Ground each prompt in concrete concepts, claims, examples, entities, relationships, qualifications, or other distinctive details from the document. Name those details directly wherever possible.

For example, a distinction in the source might motivate a comparison; a conditional claim might motivate a discussion of when it applies; a process might motivate a practical walkthrough; an easily overlooked qualification might motivate a corrective reply.

The source-specific details should determine how the new document is organized. Simply mentioning the source’s topic in an otherwise generic writing prompt is insufficient.

### Make the collection meaningfully diverse

Across the prompts, vary combinations of:

- Communicative purpose and the question or need that motivates the document.
- Organizing principle, such as mechanism, chronology, comparison, practical application, misconception, exception, consequence, or decision.
- Intended audience and assumed familiarity with the subject.
- Web context and form, such as an article, personal blog post, forum thread, reference page, FAQ, tutorial, comment, newsletter, informal notes, or another suitable form.
- Length, density, emphasis, and amount of explanation.
- Voice, authorial stance, and editorial quality.

Choose combinations that suit this source. These possibilities are a menu, not a checklist or a fixed sequence of formats.

Prompts that differ only in tone, title, or format do not provide enough diversity. Give each prompt a distinct angle on the source’s information.

### Include the range of writing quality found on the web

Include carefully edited writing, competent everyday writing, and rougher or lower-effort writing where suitable.

Rougher writing may be meandering, repetitive, abrupt, unevenly organized, overly verbose, terse, awkwardly phrased, or fragmentary. It may contain generic openings, clumsy headings, unnecessary restatements, or inconsistent levels of detail. Choose imperfections that make sense for the particular author, purpose, and web context. Random spelling errors alone are not a meaningful quality variation.

Presentation quality may vary substantially. Keep source-derived claims, factual relationships, numbers, conditions, attributions, and uncertainty faithful to the source. Preserve the status of opinions, interpretations, and disputed claims rather than converting them into established facts.

Allow illustrative scenarios and examples when useful, but distinguish them from reported events or evidence. Do not invent quotations, citations, personal experiences, experiments, or other supposed evidence.

### Vary coverage

Where the number of prompts permits, include a mixture of:

- Broad treatments that retain most of the source’s substantive content.
- Focused treatments of particular concepts, questions, examples, or details.
- Treatments that connect relevant information originally presented in different parts of the source.

Distribute attention across the collection so that major ideas and distinctive details are represented. Avoid making every prompt focus on the same most obvious points.

Each prompt should make its intended scope clear. A focused treatment must retain the qualifications and context needed to keep its selected content faithful.

### Write directly executable prompts

Address each prompt directly to the rewriting model.

Give it enough direction to establish:

- What document to write and why that document would exist.
- Which specific source material to use and emphasize.
- How to organize or transform that material.
- What audience, voice, level of polish, and approximate length to aim for.
- Any fidelity or framing constraints particularly relevant to that transformation.

Express these instructions naturally; you do not need to use the same template for every prompt.

In every prompt, instruct the rewriting model to preserve the meaning and qualifications of the source material it uses, and to return only the finished web document. The resulting document should stand on its own, without commentary about the rewriting task, references to “the provided source,” or pretraining-related framing.

Natural attribution within the document is appropriate when needed, such as referring to the report, study, policy, or argument being discussed.

### Output format

Return exactly {n} prompts.

Prefix each prompt with a Markdown heading beginning with `##`, followed by a short descriptive title. Beneath the heading, provide only the executable prompt.

Do not include an introduction, explanations of your choices, source summaries, or any of the rewritten documents.

**Example source A: A fictional library report**

Dovetail Library ran an eight-week tool-lending pilot with a collection of 30 tools. Loans lasted seven days, with a limit of two tools per borrower. The pilot recorded 160 loans and 12 late returns. Drills and sanders accounted for 90 of the loans. The report did not measure operating costs or compare the pilot with another lending program.

**Example prompts for source A:**

## A public-information page explaining the pilot

Write a compact public-information page explaining Dovetail Library’s tool-lending pilot to residents who have not read its report. Organize the page around how borrowing worked, what happened during the eight weeks, and what the results leave unanswered. Include the 30-tool collection, seven-day loans, two-tool limit, 160 loans, 12 late returns, and 90 drill-and-sander loans. Distinguish borrowing rules from observed results. Explain that operating costs and comparisons with other programs were not measured. Use helpful headings and plain, carefully edited prose. Preserve the report’s meaning and limitations. Do not invent eligibility rules, quotations, or a decision about continuing the program. Return only the finished page.

## A forum discussion about which tools to buy next

Write a short community forum thread debating whether Dovetail Library should add more drills and sanders after its pilot. Start from the fact that those two categories accounted for 90 of 160 loans. Give participants different interpretations: concentrating purchases on popular tools, maintaining a varied collection, and asking for missing cost information before deciding. Ground the discussion in the eight-week pilot and its 30-tool collection. Keep suggestions and interpretations distinct from reported findings; loan counts do not establish how many different people borrowed tools. Use short replies, occasional disagreement, and uneven writing styles. Preserve the source’s facts and limitations, and do not invent firsthand experiences or additional pilot data. Return only the finished thread.

## A repetitive local-interest blog post

Write a low-effort local-interest blog post about Dovetail Library’s tool-lending pilot. Give it a generic opening, slightly awkward subheadings, uneven paragraph lengths, and some unnecessary repetition about borrowing tools from a library. Work through the eight-week duration, 30 tools, seven-day borrowing period, two-tool limit, 160 loans, and 12 late returns. Spend disproportionate attention on the 90 loans involving drills and sanders, returning to that point more than once. Mention that operating costs and comparisons with other programs were not measured, without turning the post into a systematic evaluation. Make it sound lightly edited and somewhat padded, while keeping the facts and qualifications accurate. Do not invent personal participation or interviews. Return only the finished post.

**Example source B: Documentation for a fictional cache**

QuickCache serves cached values normally for ten minutes. After ten minutes, it tries the origin; if the origin times out, it may serve a cached value that is at most 60 minutes old. Values older than 60 minutes are never served. A successful write invalidates the written key and leaves other keys unchanged. In one repeated-read test of 1,000 requests, enabling QuickCache reduced origin reads from 1,000 to 300. That test included no writes and no concurrent clients.

**Example prompts for source B:**

## A technical FAQ for evaluating QuickCache

Write a compact technical FAQ for a developer evaluating QuickCache. Choose questions around the distinctions that matter in the documentation: normal cache use during the first ten minutes, origin requests after that point, the timeout exception allowing values up to 60 minutes old, and the prohibition on serving older values. Explain what a successful write does to the written key and to other keys. Finish with what the 1,000-request test established about origin reads and what its lack of writes and concurrent clients leaves untested. Use clear, professionally edited answers. Preserve the documented conditions and limitations. Do not invent configuration options, APIs, performance measurements, or guarantees. Return only the finished FAQ.

## A practical answer about unexpectedly old values

Write a practical troubleshooting answer to “Why did QuickCache return an old value?” Organize it around illustrative cached values aged five, 20, and 75 minutes. Explain when each could be served under the documented rules, assuming the relevant key has not been invalidated. Make the origin-timeout condition explicit for the 20-minute example and explain why the 75-minute value cannot be served. Then explain how a successful write to the same key changes the situation, without implying that a write invalidates every key. Use concise prose and a small comparison table. Preserve the source’s conditions and make clear that these are illustrative cases, not reported incidents. Return only the finished answer.

## A rough comment questioning the benchmark’s scope

Write a short, informal blog comment responding skeptically to QuickCache’s benchmark. Acknowledge that origin reads fell from 1,000 to 300 in the repeated-read test, then explain why this result alone does not establish the same improvement for workloads with writes or concurrent clients. Focus on the relationship between the tested workload and the cache’s behavior, especially the fact that successful writes invalidate the written key. Use a loosely organized argument, occasional sentence fragments, and a little repetition, as if the writer submitted the comment without much editing. Keep the skepticism faithful to the test’s stated limits. Do not invent a failed deployment, accuse the authors of dishonesty, or equate fewer origin reads with a measured latency improvement. Return only the finished comment.
